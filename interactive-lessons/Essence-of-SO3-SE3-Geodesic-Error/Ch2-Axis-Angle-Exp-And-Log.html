One Skewer, One Twist

Push a skewer through the object and twist. That is every rotation there is: one direction, one angle. How do three numbers, a point in a ball, become a rotation matrix and back?

Predict first
Read the question. Commit to an answer. Then open the card and press its button.
1 · Twist 180° about n̂. Now twist 180° about −n̂. Same rotation, or different?
The same. The plane ends in one pose. The two matrices differ by … in Frobenius norm. Opposite points on the skin of the ball are one rotation. So the skin is glued to itself.
2 · Dial 350° about +y. What does Log return?
… about …. Log always returns the shortest twist. Going 350° one way equals going 10° the other way. The dialled point is far outside the ball. The green point sits near the centre, on the opposite ray.
3 · What is the trace of R at 0°, 90° and 180°? Does the skewer's direction matter?
…, from 1 + 2 cos θ. The 1 is the skewer direction, which does not move. Each of the two turning directions adds cos θ. The direction does not matter. Aim the skewer anywhere and read "trace R" on the canvas.
Exp · skewer and twist → matrix
ω = θ n̂ K = hat(n̂) R = Exp(ω) = I + sin θ · K + (1 − cos θ) · K²
This is Rodrigues' formula. K is the "cross product with n̂" matrix. Every entry below follows the sliders.
Log · matrix → shortest skewer and twist
θ = arccos( (Tr R − 1) / 2 ) with θ ∈ [0, π] n̂ = vee(R − Rᵀ) / (2 sin θ) Log(R) = θ n̂
Why the trace reads the angle
Look along the skewer. In that view, R is a flat turn plus one fixed direction. Its diagonal reads cos θ, cos θ, 1. A change of viewpoint never changes a trace. So Tr R = 1 + 2 cos θ for every skewer.
Notation and numerics
Lowercase log gives the skew matrix θK. The ∨ operator reads its three numbers into a vector. Capital Log does both steps at once. So ‖Log(R)‖ and ‖log(R)∨‖ are the same number, θ.
Near θ = π, R − Rᵀ vanishes. The kit then reads the skewer from the symmetric part of R. Near θ = 0, arccos loses precision. The kit uses atan2(sin θ, cos θ) instead.
Sources: Solà, Deray, Atchuthan, A micro Lie theory for state estimation in robotics (2018). Huynh, Metrics for 3D Rotations: Comparison and Analysis, JMIV 2009. Park, Distance Metrics on the Rigid-Body Motions with Applications to Mechanism Design, J. Mech. Design 1995.
The Essence
One skewer, one twist. Every rotation of 3D space turns about one fixed line by one angle. That is Euler's rotation theorem. No rotation needs more.
Three numbers. ω = θ n̂ packs skewer and twist into one vector. It is a point in a solid ball of radius π. Three numbers match the three degrees of freedom of SO(3).
Exp and Log. Exp turns the point ω into the matrix R. Log goes back. Log always returns the shortest twist, with θ between 0 and π.
The trace reads the angle. Tr R = 1 + 2 cos θ, in any coordinates. That is where θ = arccos((Tr R − 1) / 2) comes from.
The skin is glued. A half turn about n̂ equals a half turn about −n̂. Opposite skin points are one rotation. So no flat map of rotation space avoids a seam.
Honest caveat. The ball is exact along rays through the centre, and distorted elsewhere. Chapter 3 uses that. To compare two rotations, first move one of them to the centre.

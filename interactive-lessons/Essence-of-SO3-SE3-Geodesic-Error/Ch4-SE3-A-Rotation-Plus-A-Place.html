SE(3): a rotation plus a place

A rotation says which way the plane points. It never says where the plane is. Add a place t and you get a pose: turn the object, then carry it.

1 · Where does the object's own origin land, in camera coordinates?
Exactly at t. The origin is p_obj = 0, and R·0 = 0, so p_cam = t. Here that is mm. So t answers "where is the object?".
2 · Is the translation of T⁻¹ equal to −t?
No. It is −Rᵀt. T⁻¹ describes the camera as seen from the object. So the walk back must be written along the object's axes. Here −Rᵀt = but −t = . Walking −t along the object's axes misses the camera by mm. They agree only when R = I.
3 · Rotate the plane about its nose. It is a pure rotation. Is t zero?
No. The motion is (Q, (I − Q)c), where c is the nose in camera coordinates. After an 80° turn its t part is , length mm. Only a turn about the origin has t = 0.

The structure

The inverse
T⁻¹ = ( Rᵀ, −Rᵀ t ) not ( Rᵀ, −t )
T⁻¹ is the camera's pose, seen from the object. So −Rᵀt is where the camera sits in the object's coordinates.

The Essence
A pose is a turn and a place. T = (R, t). Three numbers point the object. Three more place it. That makes six degrees of freedom.
Why a 4×4 matrix? So that chaining poses is plain matrix multiplication. The bottom row 0 0 0 1 makes the "+ t" happen.
Read T like a label. The columns of R are the object's axes, seen from the camera. t is the object's origin, seen from the camera.
Turn and place are coupled. Compose and you get (R_A R_B, R_A t_B + t_A). The first turn swings the second carry. So SE(3) is not SO(3) and ℝ³ side by side.
The inverse is (Rᵀ, −Rᵀt). It is the camera's pose seen from the object. −t is the same walk written in the wrong axes. They agree only when R = I.
SO(3) says where it points. SE(3) says where it points and where it is. GigaPose recovers t from the 2D shift and the scale of Mt→q; see that chapter. Caveat: R and t have different units, so a pose error needs care. Chapter 5 covers that.
